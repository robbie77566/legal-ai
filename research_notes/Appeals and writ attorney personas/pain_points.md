**Research-method note for the report writer.** This run was constrained in two ways that matter for how much weight to put on the findings: (1) the container's WebFetch and curl could not resolve or tunnel to any external host (every fetch of tidc.texas.gov, txcourts.gov, houstonlawreview.org, nacdl.org, clio.com, courts.wa.gov, opd.wa.gov, cap-la.org, bja.ojp.gov returned ENOTFOUND or a proxy 403), so no primary document was read in full; every finding below comes from search-engine result extracts of the cited page. (2) The shared per-turn web-search budget was exhausted before the last twelve planned queries ran (criminal affirmance rates, CCA habeas disposition breakdowns, out-of-time-appeal grant rates, Anders frequency in Texas, Thomson Reuters/ABA AI surveys, Westlaw/Lexis pricing, client-file ethics opinions, reporter's-record cost, AEDPA practitioner articles, TDCJ attorney-communication policy, Washington defender comment quotes, family-consultation blogs). ## Key Question 1 — Record review burden: how long does it take, what do workload standards say, how do practitioners digest a record?

### Takeaway
No Texas-specific pages-per-hour figure was found; the best quantified benchmarks are California's appointed-counsel compensation guideline (about 50 record pages per hour) and Washington's appellate caseload standard (36 appeals/year assuming a 350-page transcript, now proposed to drop to 25, with actual transcripts averaging nearly 400 pages and more exceeding 1,000). The 2023 RAND/NAPD/ABA national study covers trial-level work only and does not produce appellate case weights; Texas has a separate 2016 TIDC appellate caseload addendum whose headline number could not be extracted.

### Cited Findings

**Workload studies and standards**
- The 2023 National Public Defense Workload Study (RAND, NCSC, ABA SCLAID, Stephen Hanlon; funded by Arnold Ventures) targets "representation of adults accused of crimes in state trial courts," used a Delphi panel of 33 experts, and produced time estimates for eight activity types across eleven trial-level case types; the search extracts contain no appellate case type or weight — [National Public Defense Workload Study (ADA copy hosted by Nevada DIDS)](https://dids.nv.gov/uploadedFiles/didsnvgov/content/Meetings/2023/National%20Public%20Defense%20Workload%20Study%20-%20ADA%20Compliant%283%29.pdf); [ABA SCLAID overview of workload studies](https://dev.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/legal_aid_indigent_defendants/ls-sclaid-ind-def-overview-workload-studies.pdf)
- Colorado and Michigan appear in the NPDWS only as two of 17 states whose prior workload studies were reviewed; no Colorado or Michigan appellate caseload standard surfaced in search — [Maryland DLS Joint Chairmen's Report 2023 (survey of state standards)](https://dlslibrary.state.md.us/publications/JCR/2023/2023_4.pdf)
- Texas: TIDC published "Guidelines for Appellate Indigent Defense Caseloads" as a December 14, 2016 addendum prepared by Texas A&M's Public Policy Research Institute under HB 1318 (83rd Leg.); its table of contents includes "Caseload Recommendations Compared to Current Practice and NAC Standards" and "Final Recommended Caseload Guidelines" — [TIDC appellate addendum PDF](https://tidc.texas.gov/media/xjvdibv2/guidelines-for-indigent-defense-caseloads-appellate-addendum-12142016.pdf); companion 2015 adult guidelines — [TIDC 2015 guidelines](https://tidc.texas.gov/media/kzfd5tup/guidelines-for-indigent-defense-caseloads-01222015.pdf); NAPD description of the addendums as PPRI products supplementing the 2015 adult guidelines — [NAPD: TIDC Releases Studies on Caseloads](https://publicdefenders.us/blogs/tidc-releases-studies-on-caseloads/)
- The 1973 National Advisory Commission standard capped appellate work at "not more than ... 25 appeals" per lawyer per year — [NAPD](https://publicdefenders.us/blogs/tidc-releases-studies-on-caseloads/); [TIDC Bexar County review (2010)](https://www.tidc.texas.gov/media/pi2lyf5x/policy-monitoring-review-bexar-20100816.pdf). A critic quoted in Washington materials said the NAC figures were "informed by no data and no methodology" — [Washington OPD, SUP CrR 3.1 standard (2026)](https://opd.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/000324_SUP-CrR-3.1_Std-old.pdf)
- Washington: the 2012 court-rule standard limits appellate attorneys to 36 cases/year and "assumes experienced appellate attorneys handling cases with transcripts averaging 350 pages, and the caseload should be reduced if attorneys lack significant experience or transcripts run longer"; Washington OPD awards an additional case credit for each 800 transcript pages; WSBA has proposed an interim standard of 25 while a workload study is underway (the 2007 change had raised it from 25 to 36) — [WA OPD 2025 Appellate Caseload Standard Implementation decision package](https://opd.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/000293-2025_1T-AppellateCSI.pdf); [WA courts biennial budget copy](https://www.courts.wa.gov/content/Financial%20Services/documents/2025_2027/Biennial/96%201T%20Appellate%20Caseload%20Standard%20Implementation.pdf); [WSR 25-02-012 (Nov. 7, 2024)](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/law/wsrpdf/2025/02/25-02-012.pdf)
- Washington OPD reports that actual average transcript length is "nearly 400 pages, with more cases exceeding 1,000 pages," that the standards "don't count trial exhibits or video," and that a survey found appellate public defense attorneys "reported working on average 51 hours per week" — [WA OPD 2025 decision package](https://opd.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/000293-2025_1T-AppellateCSI.pdf)
- Oregon's appellate division averaged 65 case assignments per attorney in FY2010 ("well above recommended standards and actual practices nationwide"), falling to 51 by FY2012; Oregon's reports place national appellate PD workloads at 25–40 cases/year (Georgia, Indiana, Washington at 25; Nebraska 40), later widened to 25–50 (Florida, Louisiana at 50) — [Oregon OPDC 2011 report](https://www.oregon.gov/opdc/commission/reports/2011AnnualPerformanceProgressReport.pdf); [Oregon 2013-14 report](https://www.oregon.gov/opds/commission/reports/2013-14AnnualPerformanceProgressReport.pdf)
- New Mexico's standards also base appellate time on transcript page count — [Maryland DLS 2023](https://dlslibrary.state.md.us/publications/JCR/2023/2023_4.pdf)
- California appointed-counsel compensation guideline: record review is paid at "1.0 hour for each 60 pages of record" for pre-Oct-2004 appointments and 1 hour per 50 pages thereafter, covering "reading and reviewing the record and taking notes on facts and issues" — [California Appellate Project – LA, 2018 revised claim guidelines](https://cap-la.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/2018-rev-claim-GUIDELINES.pdf)

**Practitioner accounts of the burden**
- A Washington appellate defender's comment on the caseload rule: "the record" for the appellate public defender "is much, much larger than for the other participants on a case" — [Lila Silverstein comment (Jan. 2025)](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_Rules/proposed/2025Jan/CrR%203.1%20CrRLJ%203.1%20JuCR%209.2%201611/Lila%20Silverstein%20-%20CrR%203.1,%20CrRLJ%203.1,%20JuCR%209.2.pdf)
- A former appellate defender: an appellate attorney "cannot meet the current caseload without working nights, weekends, and while on vacation"; another commenter warns the field "will continue losing experienced attorneys to burnout and stress" — [Mary Swift comment](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_Rules/proposed/2025Jan/CrR%203.1%20CrRLJ%203.1%20JuCR%209.2%201611/Mary%20Swift%20-%20CrR%203.1,%20CrRLJ%203.1,%20JuCR%209.2.pdf); [Kate Benward comment](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_Rules/proposed/2025Jan/CrR%203.1%20CrRLJ%203.1%20JuCR%209.2%201611/Kate%20Benward%20-%20CrR%203.1,%20CrRLJ%203.1,%20JuCR%209.2.pdf) (attribution of each quote to a specific letter could not be verified because the PDFs could not be opened; both quotes come from the search extract across these comment letters)
- ACLU of Washington letter: the appellate defender "may be the only person to review the entire record" of some cases; it cites a survey in which most respondents said they had to triage work because of limited time — [ACLU comment (Jan. 2025)](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_Rules/proposed/2025Jan/CrR%203.1%20CrRLJ%203.1%20JuCR%209.2%201611/ACLU%20-%20CrR%203.1,%20CrRLJ%203.1%20and%20JuCR%209.2.pdf); a Washington appellate defender describes "a constant backlog" and asks for a lower standard — [Erin Moody comment](https://www.courts.wa.gov/court_Rules/proposed/2025Jan/CrR%203.1%20CrRLJ%203.1%20JuCR%209.2%201611/Erin%20Moody%20-%20CrR%203.1,%20CrRLJ%203.1,%20JuCR%209.2.pdf)
- A California Court of Appeal opinion on Wende (no-issue) review notes that reading a 500-plus-page transcript to look for arguable issues "took a considerable amount of time" — [California opinion (FindLaw)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1833152.html)
- How practitioners digest a record: a GMSR appellate-practice post recommends starting at the end (verdict/statement of decision, closing arguments, post-trial motions), then working backward through jury instructions, verdict forms, and pretrial rulings "where many appealable errors tend to sit," building issue lists, timelines, and a who's-who, and tracking objections and rulings closely — [GMSR, "Winning on Appeal Begins with the Record"](https://www.gmsr.com/winning-on-appeal-begins-with-the-record/); an ABA Litigation Section newsletter advises creating a consolidated single-document transcript because courts generate a separate volume per trial day — [ABA, "Tackling Complex Records"](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/resources/newsletters/appellate-practice/tackling-complex-records-appeal-begin/)
- Texas practitioner framing (Bennett & Bennett, Houston): "the trial lawyer preserves error, and the appellate lawyer finds it and frames the argument"; "more often, error is buried in a ruling that seemed right at the time, or in an instruction nobody objected to" — [Bennett & Bennett, Texas criminal appeal lawyer](https://bennettandbennett.com/texas-criminal-appeal-lawyer); a Texas firm explains the clerk's record (all filed documents) vs. the reporter's record (transcript plus admitted exhibits) — [Texas Defense Firm](https://texasdefensefirm.com/the-role-of-an-appellate-attorney-in-a-criminal-case)
- Volume context (one of 14 Texas intermediate courts): the Second Court of Appeals (Fort Worth) disposed of 395 criminal cases and received 470 new criminal cases in FY2024 — [Second COA FY2024 docket statistics](https://txcourts.gov/2ndcoa/about-the-court/docket-statistics/fy-2024/)

### Inferences
- At the California rate (50 pp/hr), a 2,000-page Texas felony record is roughly 40 hours of reading before any issue is briefed; at Washington's "nearly 400 page" average it is about 8 hours, but the Washington testimony makes clear the tail (1,000+ pages, plus un-counted exhibits and video) is where time blows up.
- Every standard found scales by transcript pages, which is evidence that page count is the practitioner-accepted proxy for appellate effort; a product that reports and triages by page/volume is speaking the workload language regulators already use.
- The Washington testimony (51-hour weeks, "nights, weekends, vacation," "constant backlog," forced triage) is the best available proxy for Texas PD appellate divisions; no Texas-specific survey surfaced.

### Gaps
- The TIDC 2016 appellate addendum's recommended annual appellate caseload and any time-per-record data could not be read (fetch blocked); it is the single most important Texas document to open next.
- No Texas practitioner statement of pages-per-hour or hours-per-record was found; no Texas appellate PD survey surfaced.
- Michigan (MIDC/SADO/MAACS) and Colorado appellate caseload standards were not found before the search budget ran out.
- Reddit practitioner accounts (r/publicdefenders, r/Lawyertalk) are inaccessible to this crawler.

## Key Question 2 — Issue spotting and preservation: how often do issues fail for lack of preservation, Anders frequency, reversal rates, and what lawyers say about missing issues

### Takeaway
No empirical Texas study of TRAP 33.1 waiver rates or Anders frequency was found; the only hard numbers are federal (Anders briefs were ~18% of federal criminal appeals in FY2010, 43% in the Southern District of Texas) and dated Texas aggregates (13% of court-of-appeals dispositions reversed at least in part in FY1993, civil and criminal combined). Texas case law shows courts routinely holding criminal issues waived for inadequate briefing or missing record cites.

### Cited Findings
- Federal benchmark: in FY2010, Anders briefs made up about 18% of 8,731 federal criminal appeals nationally; among Fifth Circuit districts, Texas Southern was 42.8% (228 of 533 appeals) and Texas Western 17.8% — [U.S. Sentencing Commission FY2010 Table 55](https://www.ussc.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/research-and-publications/annual-reports-and-sourcebooks/2010/Table55_0.pdf) (later years: [FY2013](https://www.Ussc.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/research-and-publications/annual-reports-and-sourcebooks/2013/Table55.pdf), [FY2014](https://www.ussc.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/research-and-publications/annual-reports-and-sourcebooks/2014/Table55.pdf))
- Texas CCA in High v. State (1978) complained that "far too many appeals have had to be abated by this court for non-compliance with Anders and Currie" — [High v. State, 573 S.W.2d 807 (vLex)](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/573-s-w-2d-630044086); a 2020s commentary describes the CCA "reaffirming and clarifying Anders brief procedures" — [Casemine commentary](https://www.casemine.com/commentary/us/reaffirming-and-clarifying-anders-brief-procedures-in-texas-appellate-practice/view)
- Texas reversal data (dated): in FY1993 the courts of appeals "reversed, at least in part, the decision of the trial court in 13.0 percent of the cases disposed," civil and criminal combined — [OCA Annual Report FY1993, Courts of Appeals](https://www.txcourts.gov/All_Archived_documents/JudicialInformation/pubs/ar93/coas/COA93.pdf)
- Civil-only comparison: Haynes and Boone's study of all non-criminal decisions of the 14 courts of appeals (Sept. 2018–Aug. 2019) found a 30% statewide reversal rate and 26% of appealed jury verdicts set aside — [Houston Law Review, "Reasons for Reversal in the Texas Courts of Appeals"](https://houstonlawreview.org/article/12201-reasons-for-reversal-in-the-texas-courts-of-appeals); [Haynes and Boone press release](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/press-releases/haynes-and-boone-releases-comprehensive-study-of-reversal-rates-in-texas-appeals)
- Capital direct appeals: Dow and Newberry found Texas death-row prisoners overturned conviction or sentence in 15 of 262 completed direct appeals (5.7%) — [DPIC summary](https://deathpenaltyinfo.org/news/study-reflects-increasing-futility-of-judicial-review-in-texas-death-penalty-cases)
- Inadequate briefing / missing record cites in criminal appeals: a Texas court of appeals held that an appellant's brief that "does not contain a single record reference in the entire brief" waived an ineffective-assistance complaint under TRAP 38.1(g) and Lawton v. State, 913 S.W.2d 542 — [Tex. App. opinion (FindLaw)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/crt-app-tex-eas/1753971.html); another court held a sole issue "inadequately briefs" under Rule 38.1(i) and Lucio v. State, 351 S.W.3d 878, stating courts "are not required to make the appellant's arguments for him" — [Tex. App. opinion (FindLaw)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/tx-court-of-appeals/114649454.html); [Fourteenth COA opinion applying 38.1](https://www.txcourts.gov/All_Archived_Documents/14thCOA/Case/Opinions/110101/001510f.pdf)
- TDCAA case-summary commentary: "an utter failure to follow TRAP 38.1(i) will be considered 'inadequate briefing'," which can lead to dismissal of claims "if not corrected before opinion issues" — [TDCAA case summaries, July 28, 2023](https://www.tdcaa.com/case-summaries/july-28-2023/)
- Pending CCA question (status unverified): "Is a point of error on direct appeal of a criminal case waived if harm is not briefed?" — [State Prosecuting Attorney, Hammons pending PDR](https://www.spa.texas.gov/pending-pdr-cases/hammons-houston-samuel); related SPA brief — [Zuniga](https://www.spa.texas.gov/media/gfeblvlf/zuniga.pdf)
- Texas practitioner view on why issues are missed: error is "buried in a ruling that seemed right at the time, or in an instruction nobody objected to" — [Bennett & Bennett](https://bennettandbennett.com/texas-criminal-appeal-lawyer); issues not preserved at trial "are generally waived and cannot be appealed unless they fall within narrow exceptions, such as ineffective assistance of counsel" — [Texas Defense Firm](https://texasdefensefirm.com/the-role-of-an-appellate-attorney-in-a-criminal-case)
- A UT CLE paper describes a quantitative study comparing criminal appellate opinions across Texas's courts of appeals, but the extract did not give the affirmance figure — [UT CLE paper](https://utcle.org/ecourses/get-asset-file/asset_id/54406/code/OC9148)

### Inferences
- If the Southern District of Texas federal Anders share (43%) is any guide, a large minority of appointed Texas appeals end as no-issue briefs; a product that helps counsel decide early whether a record supports a merits brief versus Anders addresses a decision point that consumes record-review hours either way (Anders still requires a full record read).
- The 38.1 waiver cases show the "cite-the-record" burden is outcome-determinative, not cosmetic: a merits issue with no record cites is treated as no issue at all.

### Gaps
- No empirical study quantifying how often Texas criminal appellate issues fail under TRAP 33.1 for lack of preservation was found.
- No Texas Anders frequency count exists in the sources reached; OCA's annual statistical reports for FY2022–FY2025 (courts of appeals criminal affirmed/reversed percentages) could not be fetched and the targeted search was cut off by the budget limit.
- The pending CCA "harm not briefed" PDR's disposition is unknown.

## Key Question 3 — Research and drafting: cost of Westlaw/Lexis for solos, free alternatives, AI hallucinations and the verification tax

### Takeaway
Texas solos have a free statewide research tier (vLex Fastcase through the State Bar, with Vincent AI only as a paid upgrade), but survey data show solo/small-firm AI adoption is shallow and anxious (only 8% of solos and 4% of small firms use AI "widely or universally"; only a third are comfortable with AI legal research), and Texas courts have repeatedly sanctioned lawyers for hallucinated citations, including in at least one criminal appellate brief.

### Cited Findings
- All State Bar of Texas members have free access to vLex Fastcase (Fastcase since 2014, expanded to all active members in 2020, migrated to vLex Fastcase in late 2024); Texas coverage includes case law, statutes, AG opinions, administrative code, and Texas practice guides; Vincent AI features require "paid upgrades" — [State Bar blog, "Coming soon: vLex Fastcase free to State Bar of Texas members" (Oct. 2024)](https://blog.texasbar.com/2024/10/articles/state-bar/coming-soon-vlex-fastcase-free-to-state-bar-of-texas-members/); [Vincent AI webinar post (May 2025)](https://blog.texasbar.com/2025/05/articles/state-bar/free-webinar-series-elevate-your-legal-research-with-vlex-fastcase-vincent-ai/); [2020 expansion](https://blog.texasbar.com/2020/01/articles/member-benefits/state-bar-of-texas-expands-partnership-with-fastcase/)
- Clio 2025 Legal Trends for Solo and Small Law Firms: only 8% of solo practitioners and 4% of small law firms have adopted AI "widely or universally" — [Clio press release](https://www.clio.com/about/press/legal-trends-solo-small-law-firms-2025/); summary: solos and small firms adopt "more cautiously than larger firms, often due to time, cost, and uncertainty" — [Wisconsin Lawyer (secondary)](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/WisconsinLawyerPDFs/99/01/9.pdf); Clio highlights post — [Clio blog](https://www.clio.com/blog/solo-small-law-firms-highlights-2025-legal-trends-2025)
- Secondary reporting on Clio's 2025 Legal Trends Report: 79% of legal professionals use AI tools, 87% at large firms, 71% at solo firms, versus 17.7% of solos in the ABA's 2024 Legal Technology Survey (the gap likely reflects question wording) — [5WPR Legal AI Visibility Report 2026 (secondary)](https://www.5wpr.com/research/legal-ai-visibility-report-2026/)
- Smokeball State of Law survey (via Law360, secondary): 53% of small/solo respondents had adopted AI software (up from 27% the prior year); only 33% were comfortable using AI-generated legal research; 39% were uneasy relying on AI outputs; about 53% raised ethical concerns — [Law360, "Small Firms Increasingly Adopting AI, But Concerns Remain"](https://www.law360.com/articles/2307327/small-firms-increasingly-adopting-ai-but-concerns-remain)
- Three-quarters of respondents in one survey said they had been hesitant to implement AI because of accuracy/ethics concerns — [2Civility/Future Law (secondary)](https://www.2civility.org/nearly-half-of-lawyers-think-ai-will-be-mainstream-in-the-legal-profession-with-three-years/); Law360 Pulse 2025 AI survey: lawyers "still worry about legal ethics and client confidentiality" even as views grow more positive — [MLex reproduction of Law360 Pulse survey](https://www.mlex.com/pulse/articles/2299612)
- Texas hallucination sanctions: Dallas Court of Appeals (June 2025) ordered an attorney to complete eight hours of CLE and pay $2,500 in opposing counsel's fees over a brief with four fake citations — [Mealey's](https://www.mealeys.com/mealeys/articles/2354256/court-orders-continuing-ed-2-500-sanction-for-texas-attorney-s-fake-ai-cites); Fifth Circuit, Fletcher v. Experian (Feb. 2026), $2,500 sanction for "hallucinated" citations in a reply brief — [Texas Lawbook](https://texaslawbook.net/fifth-circuit-sanctions-opinion-gives-practical-advice-for-ai-use/); Gauthier v. Goodyear (E.D. Tex., Nov. 2024) sanction for AI-generated nonexistent cases — [HR Daily Advisor](https://hrdailyadvisor.hci.org/?p=94808); Texas state trial court (Oct. 2025) ordered Lawrence Chang to pay $2,000 after pasting a draft into "ChatGPT's legal tool to improve his writing" — [Legal.io](https://www.legal.io/articles/5609086/Fake-Case-Citations-Land-Two-Attorneys-in-Hot-Water-Over-AI-Misuse)
- Criminal-appellate instance: Ex parte Lee (Tex. App.—Tyler 2023): the argument section was deficient because the only three published cases cited "were not real or did not support the propositions," and a footnote suggested the brief "may have been prepared by artificial intelligence (AI)" — [TDCAA case summaries, July 28, 2023](https://www.tdcaa.com/case-summaries/july-28-2023/); a Texas appeals court affirming a bail appeal noted an "illogical" brief citing nonexistent cases and apparent AI use — [Mealey's, "Fake Cites Lead Texas Court To Suspect AI's Use In Criminal Brief"](https://mealeys.com/mealeys/amp/articles/1707142)
- Court AI orders affecting Texas filers: Judge Brantley Starr (N.D. Tex., 2023) requires certification that no generative AI was used or that AI content was human-checked, with non-compliant filings stricken; E.D. Tex. local rule (effective Dec. 1, 2023) requires review and verification of computer-generated content; the Fifth Circuit proposed a Rule 32.3 AI-verification certification (adoption not confirmed in these sources) — [Mitchell Williams summary](https://www.mitchellwilliamslaw.com/taming-the-ai-beast-update-court-rules-amended-to-control-use-of-generative-ai-in-filings); [Eve Legal summary](https://www.eve.legal/blogs/what-you-need-to-know-ai-disclosure-rules-in-legal-filings); [Texas Lawbook](https://texaslawbook.net/?p=109412)
- New Jersey OPD built its own "OPD Resource Library" AI brief-drafting tool because commercial systems "were not designed for the language, legal reasoning or security requirements of public defense" — [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/new-jersey-introduces-first-of-its-kind-ai-tool-for-public-defenders); Princeton CITP's NJ BriefBank surfaces relevant appellate briefs to speed research and drafting — [Princeton CITP blog](https://blog.citp.princeton.edu/?p=19219); academic work on retrieval for public defenders — [arXiv 2601.14348](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14348v2); a 2025 interview study of public defenders' perspectives on AI adoption — [arXiv 2510.22933, "How Can AI Augment Access to Justice? Public Defenders' Perspectives on AI Adoption"](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.22933v1) (content not read; title only)

### Inferences
- Because Texas lawyers already get a free research database, a product's value for Texas solos lies less in "access to case law" than in record-grounded issue identification and verifiable citations; "free research" is table stakes in Texas.
- The sanctions trail (2024–2026) plus the Tyler court's AI footnote in a criminal habeas appeal means Texas appellate judges are primed to scrutinize AI-shaped briefs; any tool output must be verifiable against the record and reporter before it reaches a brief.

### Gaps
- Westlaw/Lexis monthly pricing for solos and practitioner statements about Casetext's shutdown, CourtListener, or Google Scholar substitution were not reached before the search budget ran out.
- The Thomson Reuters 2025 Future of Professionals and ABA TechReport 2024/2025 primary figures were not retrieved; only secondary ABA 2024 (17.7% solo AI use) is cited.
- No quantified "verification tax" (hours spent checking AI output) was found.

## Key Question 4 — Cite-checking and compliance: statements of facts with record cites, briefs struck for inadequate record cites, word-count/format rejections

### Takeaway
Texas appellate courts treat a missing record citation as a waiver of the issue (TRAP 38.1(g)/(i), Lawton, Lucio), and prosecutors' own summaries warn that an "utter failure" to follow 38.1(i) leads to dismissal of claims; no data on frequency of word-count or format rejections was found.

### Cited Findings
- Waiver for zero record references in the entire brief (criminal, ineffective-assistance claim) — [Tex. App. opinion](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/crt-app-tex-eas/1753971.html)
- "Failure to adequately brief an issue presents nothing for us to review" (Lucio v. State, 351 S.W.3d 878) — [Tex. App. opinion](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/tx-court-of-appeals/114649454.html); additional Texas applications — [Fourth COA (Alvarez, J.)](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/59ffedf1add7b0154c70f8b9); [Tex. App. opinion](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/tx-court-of-appeals/1067210.html)
- TDCAA: "an utter failure to follow TRAP 38.1(i) will be considered 'inadequate briefing'" with dismissal of claims "if not corrected before opinion issues" — [TDCAA](https://www.tdcaa.com/case-summaries/july-28-2023/)
- Fabricated citations are themselves treated as inadequate briefing (Ex parte Lee, Tyler 2023) — [TDCAA](https://www.tdcaa.com/case-summaries/july-28-2023/)

### Inferences
- Because Texas courts generally allow correction "before opinion issues," a pre-filing compliance check (every fact asserted has a volume:page cite; every case exists) directly prevents the specific waiver outcome these opinions describe.

### Gaps
- No Texas data on how often briefs are struck or returned for TRAP 9.4 word-count/format noncompliance; the planned search did not run.
- No practitioner time estimates for building a statement of facts with record cites were found.

## Key Question 5 — Deadlines: lost PDRs, late notices of appeal, restoration of appeal rights, CCA out-of-time grants

### Takeaway
The CCA routinely restores appeal rights by habeas when counsel admits a missed notice of appeal (returning the applicant to the point of filing notice, with 30 days after mandate), and the court handled roughly 2,100 non-capital 11.07 applications in FY2024, but no source quantified how many grants are out-of-time appeals or PDRs.

### Cited Findings
- CCA FY2024 (Sept. 1, 2023–Aug. 31, 2024): 2,126 regular (non-death) habeas applications added, 2,345 on docket, 2,065 disposed, 280 pending; 240 supplemental 11.07 writs added and 253 disposed; 16 death-penalty 11.071 writs added and 16 disposed — [CCA Annual Report FY24](https://txcourts.gov/media/1459678/cca-annual-reports-fy24.pdf); later and earlier years — [FY25](https://txcourts.gov/media/1462062/cca-annual-reports-fy25.pdf); [FY23](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1457424/cca-annual-reports-fy23.pdf); [FY20 (includes a disposition breakdown: relief denied, remanded, etc.)](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1450024/cca-annual-reports-fy20.pdf)
- Pattern of out-of-time-appeal grants when counsel admits fault: Ex parte Bulgier (counsel "filed late and an extension motion was never received"); Ex parte Lee (counsel gave oral notice but never filed written notice after client said he would hire other counsel); Ex parte Johnston (appellate counsel's affidavit admitted "an oversight caused the untimely notice"); Ex parte Menefee (CCA found ineffectiveness contrary to the trial court's recommendation, citing Evitts v. Lucey). Standard remedy: applicant "returned to the point where written notice can be filed," with a 30-day window after mandate — [CCA unpublished per curiam 74541](https://txcourts.gov/All_Archived_Documents/ccaInformation/archives/Opinions-Non%20Published/74541.htm); [74605](https://www.txcourts.gov/All_Archived_Documents/ccaInformation/Opinions-Non%20Published/74605.htm); [74619](https://txcourts.gov/All_Archived_Documents/ccaInformation/archives/Opinions-Non%20Published/74619.htm); [74915](https://www.txcourts.gov/All_Archived_Documents/ccaInformation/archives/Opinions-Non%20Published/74915.htm) (all 2002–2003; a small, dated, grants-only sample)
- Reporter's-record delays as a deadline driver: Texas courts of appeals cap reporter extensions (ordinarily no more than 30 days per TRAP 35.3(c); 10 days in accelerated appeals) and state that "a reporter is or has been too busy or has a heavy caseload is not sufficient" — [Eleventh COA reporter extension form](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/208078/coa11-rptreqexttime.pdf); [Seventh COA](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1449700/7th-coa-extension-request-reporters.pdf); the Twelfth COA held reporter Beverly Dixon in contempt after repeated extensions, fined her $500, and threatened jail-based work — [ABA Journal](https://abajournal.com/news/article/court-reporter-is-ordered-to-meet-deadline-or-go-to-jail-with-her-equipment-until-work-is-done); a July 2026 Thirteenth COA order abated an appeal over a reporter's failure to timely file the record — [Trevino v. State (Leagle)](https://www.leagle.com/decision/intxco20260720821); AG opinion KP-0318 (2020) on reporter shortages — [KP-0318](https://ezel.ai/ag-opinions/tx/kp-0318)

### Inferences
- The grant pattern suggests restoration is near-automatic when counsel confesses error by affidavit, which means a lost notice of appeal costs the client months to a year of delay through the 11.07 cycle rather than the appeal itself; a deadline-tracking feature prevents a predictable, embarrassing, affidavit-generating event.

### Gaps
- No statistic on how many CCA habeas grants per year are out-of-time appeals or out-of-time PDRs; the FY2020 report's disposition breakdown could not be read, and the targeted search did not run.
- No law-review data on lost PDRs or appellate-deadline malpractice in Texas was reached.

## Key Question 6 — Writ-specific pain: trial counsel files, record cost and delay, the §4 subsequent-writ bar, the AEDPA clock, intake triage, expert costs

### Takeaway
Innocence Project of Texas reports screening 186–776 new requests per half-year (and "more than 1,000 requests for assistance each year" per BJA), with initial reviews historically done by a single part-time paralegal; Texas's §4 "one bite" subsequent-writ bar is documented as the central screening constraint in Harris County PD and prosecutor materials; no sources on trial-counsel file unavailability or expert costs were reached.

### Cited Findings
- IPTX intake volumes reported to TIDC: 532 new requests (June–Dec 2022, 111 active investigations); 300 (Jan–May 2023, 97 active); 199 (June–Dec 2023, 104 active); 186 (Jan–May 2024, 93 active); 479 (Jan–May 2025, 152 active); 776 (June–Dec 2025, 106 active) — [IPTX activity report June–Dec 2022](https://tidc.texas.gov/media/rjedpct3/iptx-activity-report-june-2022-to-december-2022.pdf); [Jan–May 2023](https://tidc.texas.gov/media/aqadkwml/iptx-activity-report-january-2023-to-may-2023.pdf); [June–Dec 2023](https://tidc.texas.gov/media/jbwdxjj3/2023-jun-dec-bi-annual-report-be-revisions.pdf); [Jan–May 2025](https://www.tidc.texas.gov/media/yz2jlee5/tt-and-aandm-january-2025-to-may-2025.pdf); [June–Dec 2025](https://www.tidc.texas.gov/media/dyhfbkld/iptx-tt-and-aandm-activity-report-june-2025-to-december-2025.pdf)
- BJA award description: IPTX "receives more than 1,000 requests for assistance each year"; "initial reviews are conducted by a part-time paralegal who will soon retire"; the grant is meant to "fully and appropriately staff the intake department, reduce the case backlog" — [BJA award 15PBJA-23-GG-05241-WRNG](https://bja.ojp.gov/funding/awards/15pbja-23-gg-05241-wrng)
- Thurgood Marshall School of Law Innocence Project (a different Texas program) reported an 800-questionnaire screening backlog — [TMSLIP Jan–May 2023 report to TIDC](https://www.tidc.texas.gov/media/ycjoqj1u/tmsl-2023-jan-may-bi-annual-report.pdf)
- §4 subsequent-writ bar: Harris County Public Defender Michael Falkenberg's 11.07 paper covers "one bite at the apple," what counts as a "final disposition," and when a writ is denied versus dismissed — [UT CLE copy](https://utcle.org/elibrary/download/a/56861/p/1); [txcourts.gov copy (2023 update)](https://txcourts.gov/media/1457413/1107-paper-53123-update.pdf); prosecutors' checklist asks first whether the application "does not overcome the §4 subsequent writ bar" — [TDCAA, "A Valuable Checklist for Writs of Habeas Corpus" (2015)](https://www.tdcaa.com/journal/a-valuable-checklist-for-writs-of-habeas-corpus); repeated bills to loosen §4 (e.g., HB 3425 (86R), HB 247 (88R), HB 1291 (89R)), enactment unconfirmed — [HB 3425](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/86R/billtext/html/HB03425I.htm); [HB 247](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/billtext/pdf/HB00247E.pdf); [HB 1291](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB01291I.htm)
- Timing: Texas has no codified time limit for 11.07 writs, but federal limits (28 U.S.C. § 2244) apply and tolling depends on the state writ — [TDCAA forum thread](https://tdcaa.infopop.net/eve/forums/a/tpc/f/6050918821/m/1231057081)
- Statutory mechanics: no filing fee for habeas applications (art. 11.051); §3(d) fact development by affidavits, depositions, interrogatories, forensic testing, hearings; state pays court-ordered forensic testing except in specified circumstances — [Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 11.07 (public.law)](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._code_of_crim._proc._article_11.07); [Ch. 11 PDF](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/pdf/CR.11.pdf); CCA orders directing trial counsel to respond to IAC claims by affidavit — [Fletcher order (2020)](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1448305/wr-90-590-03-fletcher-order-7-1-20.pdf); [Zalobny order (2020)](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1448306/wr-91-030-01-02-zalobny-michael-order-7_1_2020.pdf); UT CLE overview of litigating 11.07/11.072 writs "from beginning to end" — [UT CLE 2021](https://utcle.org/ecourses/get-asset-file/asset_id/51785/code/OC8676)
- Record delay/cost (general): a NYC Bar committee letter describes waiting "for months to receive transcripts due to delays" in compiling criminal appellate records — [NYC Bar](https://www.nycbar.org/reports/delays-associated-with-compiling-the-record-on-appeal-in-criminal-cases-letter/); Minnesota's public defender ran out of money to pay for transcripts needed for appeals (2006) — [Minnesota Lawyer](https://minnlawyer.com/2006/11/06/public-defenders-out-of-transcript-money/); "without an adequate record ... there is no way for any litigant to successfully appeal" (vendor-leaning source) — [Esquire, "No Transcript, No Appeal"](https://www.esquiresolutions.com/no-transcript-no-appeal/)
- Institutional capacity: the Office of Capital and Forensic Writs is the state post-conviction defender for most capital initial habeas cases and takes "a limited number of non-capital forensic science cases" — [OCFW](https://ocfw.texas.gov/about)

### Inferences
- IPTX's 2025 intake (1,255 requests in the year, against ~100–150 active investigations) implies a screening funnel in which well over 85% of requests are declined; a structured, family-prepared pre-review would reduce screening cost per request, which the BJA grant identifies as a single-paralegal bottleneck.
- The §4 bar makes the first writ effectively the only writ, which raises the stakes of a complete record review before filing and explains practitioner reluctance to file quickly on thin records.

### Gaps
- Nothing was found on unavailability of trial counsel's file, clerk record-copy pricing, Texas reporter per-page rates, expert-witness costs in non-capital writs, or AEDPA-deadline practitioner complaints; those searches were cut off.
- Whether any §4 amendment bill was enacted is unverified.

## Key Question 7 — Economics and getting paid: appointed-fee adequacy, hours written off, flat-fee pressure, unbillable screening

### Takeaway
Texas county fee schedules reached in this run pay appointed appellate work at roughly $50–$85 per hour with hard caps of $2,500–$3,000 (versus $175/hour and a $9,700 appeal cap under federal CJA in the Northern District), and the Sixth Amendment Center has testified that Texas flat-fee systems constructively deny counsel; no data on hours written off or private flat-fee pressure in appellate practice was found.

### Cited Findings
- 81st/218th Judicial Districts (2019 fee supplement): felony flat fees of $400 (state jail/MTR) to $700 (first degree); "Appellate work is billed hourly ... at $50 per hour, with a cap of $2,500" — [81st/218th District Courts fee supplement](https://81-218.txcourts.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/2019-12-20-fee-supplement-to-indigent-defense-plan-updated.pdf)
- Tom Green County (51st District): appeals at $75/hour ($30 travel), "fees capped at $3,000" (undated) — [Tom Green County fee schedule](https://www.tomgreencountytx.gov/upload/page/0082/docs/AttyFeeSch.pdf)
- El Paso County (appointments after Jan. 1, 2024): non-capital felony, felony habeas, and revocation work at $100/hour in court and $85/hour out of court; the appellate rate (Eighth COA or CCA) appears to be $85/hour (extract truncated) — [El Paso fee schedule 2024](https://epcounty.com/courts/documents/councilofjudges/El_Paso_District_and_County_Court_Attorney_Fee_Schedule_11-2023_Uploaded_2024.pdf)
- Travis County felony fee schedule (effective May 1, 2025): "Writ Hearings $250" flat — [Travis County 2025 felony fee schedule](https://www.traviscountytx.gov/images/courts/Docs/2025_Felony_Fee_Schedule_05-01-25.pdf)
- Federal comparison: CJA rates in N.D. Tex. for 2025 are $175/hour non-capital, $223 capital, with a $9,700 appeal compensation maximum — [N.D. Tex. CJA notice](https://www.txnd.uscourts.gov/node/13965)
- Sixth Amendment Center testimony (Texas House Criminal Jurisprudence, 86th Leg.): "Constructive denial of counsel in Armstrong and Potter counties is rooted in insufficient resources and low attorney compensation. Court appointed attorneys are paid a single flat fee, in most cases, without regard to how much or how little time the attorney must devote to that case" (about $400–$500 for a misdemeanor or state jail felony) — [6AC testimony handout](https://www.legis.state.tx.us/tlodocs/86R/handouts/C2202020102200003/2894ab83-c1fb-4042-bd69-bfa7c252d585.PDF)
- NACDL, Rationing Justice (2013): fee caps "result in attorneys earning less per hour the more they work on a client's case," creating "a financial disincentive" and "a conflict of interest" — [NACDL Rationing Justice](https://static.prisonpolicy.org/scans/Rationing-Justice.pdf)
- Historical habeas-fee adequacy debate: 1995 state-paid capital habeas counsel with CCA hour caps that some called inadequate ("250 hours was more realistic") — [Christian Science Monitor (1995)](https://proof.csmonitor.com/1995/1220/20031.html); 2007 House analysis creating the Office of Capital Writs cited poor quality of appointed habeas representation — [SB 1655 analysis](https://lrl.texas.gov/scanned/hroBillAnalyses/80-0/SB1655.PDF); DOJ's 2018 letter on Texas's capital-counsel mechanism noted commenters' view that compensation "was too low to attract and retain competent counsel" — [DOJ letter (2018)](https://www.justice.gov/d9/pages/attachments/2018/06/20/2018-6-19_doj_letter_to_texas.pdf)
- Harris County indigent appointments report (2023) exists but its content was not reached — [Texas CJE](https://texascje.org/sites/default/files/2025-11/Harris%20County%20Indigent%20Appointments%20Report%202023.pdf)

### Inferences
- At $50–$85/hour with a $2,500–$3,000 cap, a Texas appointed appeal pays for roughly 30–60 hours; at the California 50-pages/hour reading rate, a 2,000-page record alone consumes most or all of that cap before drafting. This is the structural reason appellate counsel triage, and it is a direct quantification of the time a review tool would need to save to matter economically.
- Hourly-with-cap schedules replicate the flat-fee disincentive NACDL and 6AC criticize: every hour past the cap is written off.

### Gaps
- No Texas data on hours written off per appeal, private flat-fee pricing for criminal appeals/writs, or time spent on unbillable family screening was found; the planned searches did not run.
- TIDC statewide appellate expenditure or average-cost-per-appeal data were not retrieved.

## Key Question 8 — Communication: incarcerated clients and families, prison mail/phone limits, what lawyers wish families brought

### Takeaway
No Texas-specific (TDCJ) attorney-communication source was reached; general bar reports document two-week-plus legal mail delays, month-long waits to schedule confidential calls, and months-long inability to reach clients in federal facilities. Nothing was found on what Texas appellate lawyers wish families brought to a first consultation.

### Cited Findings
- NYCLA/ABA report (2015): "it can take two weeks or more for an inmate to receive postal mail sent from an attorney, and most prisons do not accept expedited mail delivery"; scheduling a confidential call "must ordinarily be initiated by the inmate, and can take up to a month to complete" — [NYCLA/ABA resolution report](https://www.law.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/archive/2021/02/CrimDivisionRecord-NYCLA-ABAResolutionReport-11102015.pdf)
- NACDL letter to DOJ/BOP (Aug. 2023): attorneys "have experienced an inability to obtain legal calls with their clients for months at a time"; facilities "do not answer the phone and fail to respond to email"; some calls are not confidential because staff remain in the room — [NACDL letter](https://www.nacdl.org/getattachment/6c0b6470-3ba2-44a8-8333-6befb1eb4d14/nacdl-letter-to-dag-and-bop-on-access-to-counsel-in-bop-pretrial-and-other-facilities-08292023.pdf)
- Richmond Law Review (Feb. 2025), "We Need to Talk: Modernizing Attorney-Client Jail Communications" — [Richmond Law Review](https://lawreview.richmond.edu/2025/02/28/we-need-to-talk-modernizing-attorney-client-jail-communications/) (content not read)
- Texas jail example: a lawsuit alleges Securus recorded attorney-client conversations at the Travis County Jail and passed video to prosecutors (allegation, county jail, not TDCJ) — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/video-only-prison-visits-to-be-introduced-in-texas-jails); legal-mail copying policies that let staff view privileged material — [ACLU of Arizona](https://www.acluak.org/news/doc-denying-access-justice-over-and-over-and-over-again/); a Washington family's court notice delayed more than two months because it "did not qualify as legal mail" — [The Appeal](https://theappeal.org/washington-prisons-appeals-court-efiling/); California appellate defender client-communication guide (2026) — [FDAP CDCR guide](https://www.fdap.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/CDCR-Client-Communications-Guide.pdf)

### Inferences
- If a Texas writ lawyer needs a month to schedule one confidential call and two weeks per mail exchange, the family is the practical real-time channel; a family-facing pre-review report (organized judgment, docket, record volumes, trial-counsel contact, deadlines) would let the first consultation start from facts rather than from the lawyer's own record-gathering. This is inference; no source documented the practice.

### Gaps

- No source on what appellate/writ lawyers wish families brought to intake, or on time spent fielding family update requests.

## Key Question 9 — Adoption attitudes: what these lawyers want from AI and what they fear; PD use of AI for discovery review

### Takeaway
National defender organizations have moved from caution to a stated "duty" framing (NACDL's July 2026 "Parity in Practice" white paper with a model AI-use policy), and defender offices are using AI mainly for video/bodycam discovery review (Kentucky DPA, Colorado SPD with Reduct); surveys still show confidentiality, ethics, and hallucination as the dominant fears, and no Harris County or Travis County PD AI pilot was found.

### Cited Findings
- NACDL released "Parity in Practice: The Defender's Duty to Ethically Use AI" (board-approved Feb. 2026, released July 2026), "a framework for ethical and practical AI use across all types of criminal defense practice," with "a model AI Use Policy ... in Appendix A"; it cites the Kentucky Department of Public Advocacy using AI software to review a surge of bodycam footage — [NACDL news release](https://www.nacdl.org/newsrelease/News-Release-~-Parity-in-Practice); [NACDL white paper page](https://www.nacdl.org/ParityInPractice); coverage framing it as a "duty to use AI" — [Minnesota Lawyer (Aug. 2026)](https://minnlawyer.com/2026/08/04/nacdl-criminal-defense-lawyers-duty-to-use-ai/)
- Colorado State Public Defender reportedly uses Reduct to pull clips and build exhibits from bodycam footage; NAPD programmed a May 2025 "Better technology than the DAs have: AI-assisted workflows for public defense" session and a September 2026 two-part AI training — [NAPD 2025 Changemakers Forum session](https://napd2025changemakersforum.sched.com/event/1t0w5/reduct-spark-session-better-technology-than-the-das-have-ai-assisted-workflows-for-public-defense); [NAPD Virtual Tech Expo 2026](https://napdvirtualtechexpo2026.sched.com/event/2QX9O)
- Council on Criminal Justice (Sept. 2026) published a hypothetical case study on public defender use of general-purpose AI for research, document review, motion drafting, discovery analysis, and case preparation, noting "hallucinations, bias, and breaches of confidentiality" as concerns — [CCJ case study](https://counciloncj.org/assessing-ai-in-action-a-case-study-on-public-defender-use-of-general-purpose-ai-tools/)
- New Jersey OPD's purpose-built brief tool and the stated reason (commercial tools not built for PD "language, legal reasoning or security requirements") — [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/new-jersey-introduces-first-of-its-kind-ai-tool-for-public-defenders)
- Fear/comfort data: only 33% of small/solo lawyers comfortable with AI legal research, 39% uneasy relying on outputs, 53% with ethical concerns (Smokeball via Law360) — [Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2307327/small-firms-increasingly-adopting-ai-but-concerns-remain); ethics and client confidentiality persist as top worries (Law360 Pulse 2025) — [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/pulse/articles/2299612); 8% of solos / 4% of small firms using AI widely (Clio 2025) — [Clio](https://www.clio.com/about/press/legal-trends-solo-small-law-firms-2025/)
- Court-imposed verification duties (N.D. Tex. Starr order; E.D. Tex. rule; proposed Fifth Circuit 32.3) and the string of Texas sanctions (see KQ3) — [Mitchell Williams](https://www.mitchellwilliamslaw.com/taming-the-ai-beast-update-court-rules-amended-to-control-use-of-generative-ai-in-filings); [Texas Lawbook on Fifth Circuit sanctions](https://texaslawbook.net/fifth-circuit-sanctions-opinion-gives-practical-advice-for-ai-use/)
- A "2024 pilots" post claiming measured time savings and hallucination rates for PD AI use cites no primary sources and should not be relied on — [DEV Community post (unreliable)](https://dev.to/courtgpt/public-defender-workload-and-ai-empirical-patterns-from-2024-pilots-1d3b)

### Inferences
- Defender-side AI adoption so far centers on volume problems with low hallucination risk (video/bodycam clipping, brief banks), not on generating legal argument; a record-review product fits that pattern if its outputs are record-anchored pointers (volume, page, line, the objection and the ruling) rather than drafted argument.
- NACDL's "duty" framing plus explicit court verification orders create a two-sided pressure: offices are being told they must use AI competently and that they will be sanctioned if it is wrong, which makes verifiability the purchase criterion.

### Gaps
- No evidence of Harris County PD, Travis County PD, or any Texas PD AI pilot was found (one targeted search, no hits).
- The NACDL white paper's full text, the arXiv PD-perspectives interview study, and the CCJ case study were not readable; no verbatim practitioner quotes about AI fears specific to appellate/writ lawyers were obtained.
- Thomson Reuters, LexisNexis, Bloomberg Law, and ABA TechReport primary survey figures were not retrieved before the budget limit.
